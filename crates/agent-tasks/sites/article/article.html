The Kestrel Bay Bridge

The Kestrel Bay Bridge carries the coast road across the mouth of the Alder River, where the river meets the sea between two low headlands. For most of a century it has been the only fixed crossing of the estuary, and it is the reason the towns on either bank grew into one another instead of apart. This page tells its story, from the ferries that came before it to the repairs that keep it standing today.

Before the bridge

For two hundred years the only way across the estuary was the ferry. A flat-bottomed boat, pulled along a chain, carried carts, cattle and people from the north landing to the south landing in about twenty minutes when the tide was kind and in more than an hour when it was not. In winter storms the ferry often did not run at all, and farmers on the north bank had to drive their animals twelve miles inland to the nearest ford. Markets were missed, letters were late, and a doctor called from the far side might arrive too late to help.

The idea of a bridge was raised many times. Merchants wanted it for trade, the county wanted it for the new motor road, and the families on the north bank wanted it simply so that they would no longer be cut off for days at a time. Every proposal stumbled on the same two problems: the soft mud of the river bed, which seemed unable to hold a heavy pier, and the cost, which no single town could carry alone.

Building it

The plans that were finally accepted came from a young engineer named Ruth Calloway, who proposed a steel arch with only two piers, each sunk through the mud onto the rock far below. Work began in the spring of 1928 and took four years. Hundreds of workers came from across the region, and a small village of huts grew up on the south headland to house them. The bridge was opened to traffic in 1932, on a cold morning in late October, when the first car crossed to the cheers of a crowd that had walked out from both towns to watch.

Sinking the piers was the hardest part of the work. Each one was built inside a caisson, a huge open box of steel that was floated into place and then slowly pressed down into the mud as men dug out the ground beneath it. The men worked in compressed air to keep the water out, and they could only stay down for short shifts. Several of them suffered from the bends, and the records of the time name two workers who lost their lives during the construction. A plaque on the south pier remembers them.

The arch itself went up much faster. Its two halves were built out from the piers, piece by piece, held back by cables until they met above the middle of the channel. People came from far away on Sundays to watch the gap close, and local newspapers printed a photograph of the last rivet each week until the arch was complete. When the two halves finally touched, they were less than a finger's width out of line.

A working bridge

Once it was open, the bridge changed life on the estuary almost at once. The ferry made its last crossing within a month, and the chain that had pulled it was lifted from the river and sold for scrap. Lorries began to carry fish from the harbour on the north bank to the railway on the south bank, and a bus service linked the two towns every hour. Shops that had served only one side of the river found customers from both.

The bridge has not always had an easy life. During a great storm in 1957 a fishing boat broke from its moorings and struck the north pier, cracking the stone facing and closing the road for three weeks. Salt spray eats at the steel, and the arch has to be painted again every few years. Traffic has also grown far beyond anything its builders imagined: the bridge was designed for horses, carts and a few cars, and it now carries thousands of vehicles every day.

The bridge today

A major restoration in 1988 replaced the road deck, strengthened the arch and added a separate path for people on foot and on bicycles. The work was planned so that the bridge would look just as it did when it was new, and the steel was painted in the same deep green that Ruth Calloway had chosen. Engineers inspect the piers every year, and divers check the caissons for any sign that the river is wearing the mud away around them.

Today the bridge is a protected monument as well as a busy road. Walkers cross it for the view of the bay, photographers wait on the headlands for the evening light on the arch, and a small museum in the old ferry house on the north landing tells the story of the crossing, with the ferry bell, the engineer's drawings and a model of the caissons. On the anniversary of the opening each autumn, the towns on both banks close the bridge to traffic for an afternoon and hold a fair on the road deck, with music, food stalls and a walk across for everyone who wants to join it.
